Change of Basis¶

Definition¶

Let VVV be an nnn-dimensional real vector space with ordered bases B=(b1,…,bn)\mathcal B=(\boldsymbol{b}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{b}_n)B=(b1​,…,bn​) and C=(c1,…,cn)\mathcal C=(\boldsymbol{c}_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol{c}_n)C=(c1​,…,cn​). The coordinate vector [x]B∈Rn[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B}\in\mathbb R^n[x]B​∈Rn lists the unique scalars with x=∑i([x]B)i bi\displaystyle \boldsymbol{x}=\sum_i([\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B})_i\,\boldsymbol{b}_ix=i∑​([x]B​)i​bi​.

The change-of-basis matrix from B\mathcal BB to C\mathcal CC is the n×nn\times nn×n matrix

PC←B=([b1]C⋯[bn]C),[x]C=PC←B[x]B  for all x∈V.\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}=\begin{pmatrix}[\boldsymbol{b}_1]_{\mathcal C}&\cdots&[\boldsymbol{b}_n]_{\mathcal C}\end{pmatrix}, \qquad [\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal C}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B}\ \text{ for all }\boldsymbol{x}\in V.PC←B​=([b1​]C​​⋯​[bn​]C​​),[x]C​=PC←B​[x]B​  for all x∈V.

In Rn\mathbb R^nRn with C\mathcal CC the standard basis, write B=(b1⋯bn)\boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{b}_1&\cdots&\boldsymbol{b}_n\end{pmatrix}B=(b1​​⋯​bn​​). Then x=B[x]B\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{B}[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B}x=B[x]B​ and [x]B=B−1x[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}[x]B​=B−1x.

Interpretation¶

The vector x\boldsymbol{x}x does not move; only its description changes. The matrix PC←B\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}PC←B​ translates "B\mathcal BB-language" into "C\mathcal CC-language", and its columns are the old basis vectors written in the new coordinates.

The figure shows the first example. The green arrow x=(3,1)T\boldsymbol{x}=(3,1)^Tx=(3,1)T is one fixed vector. Along the standard axes it is 3e1+e23\boldsymbol{e}_1+\boldsymbol{e}_23e1​+e2​; along the tilted grid of B\mathcal BB it is 2b1+b22\boldsymbol{b}_1+\boldsymbol{b}_22b1​+b2​. Changing basis only changes which grid the vector is read on, so its coordinates change from (3,1)T(3,1)^T(3,1)T to [x]B=B−1x=(2,1)T[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}=(2,1)^T[x]B​=B−1x=(2,1)T.

The direction of the arrow is the usual source of errors. The matrix whose columns are the new basis vectors in standard coordinates converts new coordinates into standard ones, so going from standard to new coordinates needs its inverse. Texts differ in which of the two they call "the" transition matrix; the course's convention has not been checked against the slides.

Changing basis in both domain and codomain changes the matrix representation of a linear map. For an operator on one space the result is a similarity transformation; choosing an eigenbasis makes the matrix diagonal.

Key Results¶

  • PC←B\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}PC←B​ is invertible and PC←B−1=PB←C\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal B\leftarrow\mathcal C}PC←B−1​=PB←C​; it is the matrix [I]C,B[\boldsymbol{I}]_{\mathcal C,\mathcal B}[I]C,B​ of the identity map. Conversely, every invertible n×nn\times nn×n matrix is the change-of-basis matrix from the basis formed by its columns to the standard basis.
  • Composition: PD←B=PD←CPC←B\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal D\leftarrow\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal D\leftarrow\mathcal C}\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}PD←B​=PD←C​PC←B​. In Rn\mathbb R^nRn, PC←B=C−1B\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{C}^{-1}\boldsymbol{B}PC←B​=C−1B.
  • For a linear operator T\boldsymbol{T}T with S=PC←B\boldsymbol{S}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C\leftarrow\mathcal B}S=PC←B​: [T]B=S−1[T]CS[\boldsymbol{T}]_{\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{S}^{-1}[\boldsymbol{T}]_{\mathcal C}\boldsymbol{S}[T]B​=S−1[T]C​S.
  • For a linear map T ⁣:V→W\boldsymbol{T}\colon V\to WT:V→W with bases changed in both spaces, [T]C′,B′=PC′←C[T]C,BPB←B′[\boldsymbol{T}]_{\mathcal C',\mathcal B'}=\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal C'\leftarrow\mathcal C}[\boldsymbol{T}]_{\mathcal C,\mathcal B}\boldsymbol{P}_{\mathcal B\leftarrow\mathcal B'}[T]C′,B′​=PC′←C​[T]C,B​PB←B′​, where B,B′\mathcal B,\mathcal B'B,B′ are bases of VVV, C,C′\mathcal C,\mathcal C'C,C′ are bases of WWW, and [T]C,B[\boldsymbol{T}]_{\mathcal C,\mathcal B}[T]C,B​ maps B\mathcal BB-coordinates to C\mathcal CC-coordinates.
  • If B\mathcal BB is orthonormal in Rn\mathbb R^nRn, then B−1=BT\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}=\boldsymbol{B}^TB−1=BT and ([x]B)i=biTx([\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B})_i=\boldsymbol{b}_i^T\boldsymbol{x}([x]B​)i​=biT​x.

Examples¶

Coordinates. In R2\mathbb R^2R2 let B=((1,1)T,(1,−1)T)\mathcal B=((1,1)^T,(1,-1)^T)B=((1,1)T,(1,−1)T), so B=(111−1)\boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}B=(11​1−1​) and B−1=12(111−1)\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}B−1=21​(11​1−1​). For x=(3,1)T\boldsymbol{x}=(3,1)^Tx=(3,1)T,

[x]B=B−1x=12(4,2)T=(2,1)T,[\boldsymbol{x}]_{\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{x}=\tfrac12(4,2)^T=(2,1)^T,[x]B​=B−1x=21​(4,2)T=(2,1)T,

and indeed 2(1,1)T+1(1,−1)T=(3,1)T2(1,1)^T+1(1,-1)^T=(3,1)^T2(1,1)T+1(1,−1)T=(3,1)T.

An operator. Let A=(2112)\boldsymbol{A}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}A=(21​12​) in standard coordinates. Then AB=(313−1)\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}=\begin{pmatrix}3&1\\3&-1\end{pmatrix}AB=(33​1−1​) and

[A]B=B−1AB=12(6002)=(3001).[\boldsymbol{A}]_{\mathcal B}=\boldsymbol{B}^{-1}\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}=\tfrac12\begin{pmatrix}6&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.[A]B​=B−1AB=21​(60​02​)=(30​01​).

In the basis B\mathcal BB the map stretches the first coordinate by 333 and leaves the second alone: B\mathcal BB is an eigenbasis, and this is a diagonalization of A\boldsymbol{A}A.
